The specific decklist beats the archetype label, by a small real margin

Outside the quantum-computing focus of the rest of this site - I started playing Magic (Marvel Super Heroes precons pulled me in) and wanted to know how a specific deck I'm building would actually perform, and what to change about it, not "Red Aggro is at 54% this week." A personal-interest project, reported with the same discipline as the rest of this site.

Short version: predicting a real MTGO Standard decklist's tournament performance from its actual card list beats predicting from its archetype label alone - archetype-only gets R²=0.018, reading the real card list gets R²=0.028 - and that gap is real, confirmed two separate ways rather than reported on one number. It is also small. This isn't a working win-rate predictor; it's evidence that the card list itself carries signal an archetype tag can't.

The methodological trap, avoided before writing any code

The obvious data source for "deck vs. deck matchup performance" is a site like magicmeta.net's Standard Matchup Matrix - real, structured, archetype-vs-archetype win rates. Using it as a training label would have made the entire project circular: if every deck tagged "Red Aggro" gets Red Aggro's archetype-level win rate as its label, a model wins by reconstructing the archetype and looking up its rate, which proves nothing about reading the actual 60 cards. Two other candidate sources, aetherhub.com and mtgdecks.net, both declare Content-Signal: ai-train=no in their robots.txt, checked directly before writing a scraper - ruled out on that basis regardless of the circularity question.

What's used instead: mtgo.com's own official event pages, which embed real, individual decklists joined to real per-player standings (rank, game win %) by login ID - a specific list's specific real result, not an archetype average. 417 real Standard Challenge events, September 2025 through August 2026, self-rate-limited well past what its (nonexistent) robots.txt would require. 13,036 decklists, every one of them joined to a real outcome.

A data anomaly that turned out to be structurally real, not corrupted

Resolving MTGO's card names against Scryfall left 4.13% of all card references unmatched. Most of that (98 of 156 distinct missing names) turned out to be a simple naming-convention gap: MTGO reports only a split/adventure/double-faced card's front face (Cheeky House-Mouse), where Scryfall keys the full card (Cheeky House-Mouse // Squeak By) - fixed with a front-face index.

What was left, 57 distinct card names all tagged with MTGO's internal OM1 cardset code, didn't yield to that fix, so it got investigated properly instead of shrugged off. A live Scryfall search for any of these names - Kavaero, Mind-Bitten, Bayo, Irritable Instructor, Rizna, the Spider-Crowned - returns zero results, not just absent from a local snapshot but absent from Scryfall's live database and its autocomplete entirely, confirmed against a same-day re-download. Scryfall does have a real set coded om1 ("Through the Omenpaths," a digital Spider-Man exclusive with genuine Marvel names like Agent Venom) - but MTGO's OM1-tagged cards are a thematically coherent, unrelated spider/web-themed set (Leyline Weaver, Withar, Cocoon Keeper) with a sane five-color, full-rarity, 0-5 mana curve spread across 92% of events and 61% of decks, a few cards per deck at a time - the structural signature of a real, complete set, not corrupted data. Most likely an MTGO-internal set-code collision with Scryfall's own om1, not the same product under a formatting mismatch. Resolved pragmatically: for the ~3% of card slots this touches, mana cost, color, and card type - the features this project actually needs - come directly from MTGO's own card attributes instead of Scryfall, so nothing downstream loses information over it.

Archetypes had to be invented, honestly

MTGO's raw data has no archetype or deck-type field at all - checked directly against the JSON keys, not assumed missing. Labels are inferred by TF-IDF-weighted K-means clustering over each deck's nonland card-inclusion vector (k=20, chosen by silhouette score), the same general technique real metagame-analysis tools use. These are cluster IDs, referred to as inferred clusters everywhere in this project, never presented as official archetype names - a burn shell full of Nova Hellkite and Lightning Strike, a tempo shell full of Sleight of Hand and Slickshot Show-Off, and seventeen more, none of them named by a human.

5-fold cross-validation, grouped by event - sibling decklists from the same tournament share a metagame and field strength, and a random split would let a model partly learn "this event's field was weak" instead of "this decklist is good."

R² improves by +0.0099. Before trusting that as "the model reads the decklist," it has to survive being checked, not just reported:

Is it a modeling-capacity artifact? A nonlinear model (HistGradientBoosting) on the identical features gets R²=0.0238 - worse than the linear one. A more expressive model doesn't find more signal sitting there; the effect really is this small, regardless of model family.

Does it discriminate within an archetype in a way that matches reality? For every deck, its deviation from its own cluster's mean outcome, real result against model prediction, correlated across all 13,036 decks: r=0.0889 (p=2.60e-24). Real and about as statistically unambiguous as a correlation gets - but the effect size is small, and that p-value mostly reflects the sample size, not the strength of the relationship, worth saying plainly rather than letting the exponent imply more than the r does. Per cluster, the picture is less clean: most of the 19 clusters with at least 30 decks show a positive within-cluster correlation, up to r=0.19 in the strongest one, but five show a negative one - the model's within-cluster ranking runs the wrong direction there.

What this actually says

The specific card list carries real signal beyond the archetype label - confirmed two ways, not accepted on one number - and it's a small effect. Predicting an individual MTGO decklist's tournament performance from its card list isn't a solved problem at this data volume, and this project doesn't claim otherwise. What it demonstrates is narrower: the list itself does *some* of the work an archetype label alone can't, which was the actual question, not "build an accurate win-rate predictor."

The swap search

Built on top of the trained model anyway, because it's the part closest to actually new and the honesty checks above earned it: single-card substitutions searched per nonland slot, matched by supertype, mana cost within 1, and color identity a subset of the deck's own, restricted to genuine Scryfall Standard-legal cards (the OM1 fallback slice has no legality list, so it's excluded as a swap target rather than guessed at). Runs in under a second per deck against a ~4,600-card legal pool. Tried on the highest-win-rate real decklist in the corpus, a Sultai Vivi Ornitier shell that went 100%: top suggestion is Vivi Ornitier -> Flamewake Phoenix, predicted delta +0.0084. Small, on purpose - a tool built on a weak-but-real model should recommend weak improvements, not confident ones it can't back up.
